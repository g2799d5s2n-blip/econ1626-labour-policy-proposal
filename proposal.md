# AI and the Australian Labour Market: A Compositional Approach

**Policy brief prepared for the Commissioner, Jobs and Skills Australia**
**Submission to the Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence**

---

## Executive Summary

Artificial intelligence is not producing aggregate job losses in Australia. It is producing a recomposition of employment that aggregate indicators are poorly equipped to detect.

Exposure research — ILO Working Paper 140 (2025) and the OECD AI Capability Gap index (2026) — identifies transformation rather than displacement as the dominant mechanism. DEWR (2026) finds no economy-wide disruption, and reports employment among 20–24 year olds as more resilient than among those aged 25 and over since late 2022. Neither finding rules out contraction at entry level in exposed occupations: an age-based aggregate pools sectors moving in opposite directions, including derived demand from AI infrastructure, where 13,000 of the 72,000 additional energy trades workers required by 2030 are linked to data centres. Aggregate resilience may be a signature of recomposition rather than evidence against it.

Compositional problems require targeted instruments. Yet the 2026–27 Budget narrows employer apprenticeship incentives without reference to AI exposure. This proposal recommends adding that criterion rather than creating new programs.

---

## Problem Definition & Context

On 20 August 2026 the Australian Parliament established a Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence, due to report on 30 November 2026, creating an immediate window in which labour market evidence can shape Commonwealth policy settings.

The problem is one of measurement as much as of impact: AI reshapes Australian work through distinct channels that current indicators capture unevenly. The first concerns whether AI automates or augments tasks within existing occupations. The second concerns wages, where exposure attracts a premium rather than a penalty. The third — the focus of this brief — concerns sectoral restructuring and the compression of entry-level recruitment, alongside derived demand for manual trades from AI infrastructure.

Each carries distributional consequences that national aggregates obscure, across regions, age groups and gender.

---

## Analysis of Impacts

### Task automation and augmentation

Two independent approaches converge. The ILO's refined index scores exposure at task level across ISCO-08 occupations (Gmyrek et al. 2025); the OECD maps nine AI capability domains against occupational requirements to construct a capability gap (OECD 2026). Despite differing methods, both identify transformation rather than wholesale substitution as the dominant pattern, with clerical and routine information-processing work most exposed. Jobs and Skills Australia reaches a broadly consistent view for Australia.

### Wages

Exposure currently attracts a premium rather than a penalty. PwC (2026) reports a wage premium of 62 per cent for AI-skilled roles, up from 57 per cent, with AI-related advertisements rising from 20,000 to 41,000 across 2024–25. The premium is itself distributional: it widens the gap between workers who can access AI training and those who cannot.

### Sectoral restructuring and entry-level compression

The aggregate evidence supports a compositional reading more than it first appears. DEWR (2026) finds no broad AI-driven upheaval, but reports employment in the most AI-exposed occupations growing 5.6 per cent since November 2022, against 9.5 per cent in the least exposed — an early indication of modest slowing rather than proof of job loss.

Firm-level evidence is sharper. Jobs and Skills Australia (2025) records employers shifting recruitment toward senior and experienced workers and away from junior staff. ReadyTech reported hiring fewer entry-level engineers as it deployed genAI coding assistants, shifting its junior–senior composition; a senior leader saw no business case for training junior engineers as before. JSA concludes that entry-level roles may be more likely to transform than diminish, while the technology sector may be among the first to restructure its entry-level intake.

Employment among 20–24 year olds has nonetheless grown faster than among those aged 25 and over. Age-based aggregates pool divergent sectoral trajectories; the absence of a youth-level signal is largely uninformative about occupational contraction.

### Derived demand from AI infrastructure

Skilled manual trades record low direct exposure on task-based measures. Low exposure is not insulation: it means AI does not perform these tasks, not that demand for them is unchanged.

The mechanism runs through physical infrastructure. Deloitte Access Economics (2026) tracks 33 Australian data centre projects worth $104 billion, a fourfold increase in a single year. The Powering Skills Organisation (2026) estimates data centre construction and operations could require an additional 13,000 energy trades workers by 2030, within a total shortfall of 72,000 — against a Skill Level 3 vacancy fill rate of 55.1 per cent (Jobs and Skills Australia 2026).

AI is not reducing demand for skilled manual labour; it is relocating and intensifying it through the infrastructure the technology requires.

### Distributional consequences

The channels do not fall evenly. Exposure concentrates in clerical and routine information-processing work rather than in occupations requiring non-standardised physical activity, and carries a gender profile: men make up 43.7 per cent of Australia's most-exposed group but close to 70 per cent of the least exposed (DEWR 2026), consistent with the ILO's finding of higher female exposure in high-income economies (Gmyrek et al. 2025).

Regional variation is material — JSA publishes employment-region data — but the trades shortage is national in scope.

## Policy Options

### Option 1: Incorporate AI exposure into the Apprenticeships Priority List methodology

**Mechanism.** The 2026–27 Budget revises the Priority List methodology and incentive rates from 1 January 2027, redirecting employer incentives toward small and medium employers and Group Training Organisations. That redirection operates on firm size and existing priority occupations, not AI exposure. This option adds exposure as a criterion, making entry-level placements in highly exposed occupations eligible.

**Target.** Employers hiring first-year entrants in exposed clerical, administrative and technical roles.

**Cost.** No new appropriation: the criterion applies within the reformed structure commencing 1 January 2027, which narrows eligibility and returns $266.2 million over four years ($106.3 million annually thereafter) to budget.

**Assessment.** Efficient, since the methodology is already being rewritten, and favourable on equity given female concentration in exposed occupations. Two constraints bind: timing, as the Committee reports weeks before commencement; and dilution, since adding eligible occupations without new appropriation trades off against existing priority trades.

### Option 2: Establish recurrent AI exposure monitoring within Jobs and Skills Australia

**Mechanism.** Convert JSA's Generative AI Capacity Study from a one-off exercise into a standing annual series, combining occupational exposure scores with the Internet Vacancy Index and reporting entry-level vacancy trends disaggregated by region, age and sex. DEWR's monitoring framework already draws on both inputs; this gives it a recurring mandate and published cadence.

**Target.** Not workers directly, but the institutions setting policy: JSA, DEWR, Treasury and future parliamentary inquiries.

**Cost.** Approximately $15–25 million annually, against the $9.1 million per year JSA already receives for its legislated labour market advice.

**Assessment.** Highly feasible: the analytical infrastructure exists and requires extension rather than construction. Its weakness is directness — it improves future decisions without altering any current outcome. A complement to Option 1, not a substitute.

### Option 3: Co-fund a data centre trades training pathway with industry

**Mechanism.** The constraint here is training capacity, not demand. The Powering Skills Organisation (2026) reports 72 per cent of training providers operating at or above capacity for electrotechnology students, while 40 per cent have no plans to expand infrastructure. AirTrunk's programme — thirty mechanical and electrical apprentices from May 2024, four years to a TAFE qualification, supplemented by the DCD>Academy programme — demonstrates a workable model at small scale. This option co-funds its expansion, with Commonwealth contribution matched by participating operators and delivered through state TAFE systems.

**Target.** New entrants to electrical and mechanical trades, oriented toward data centre construction and operation.

**Cost.** Approximately $40–60 million over four years.

**Assessment.** Well targeted, with the capacity constraint documented rather than assumed. Feasibility is the weakness: VET delivery is a state responsibility and the model depends on operators sustaining co-investment. Deadweight loss is a residual risk, since operators already recruit and train without Commonwealth support.

---

## Recommended Package & Implementation Plan

**Recommendation.** Adopt Options 1 and 2 as an integrated package, with Option 3 as a conditional pilot. The training-capacity evidence strengthens the case for Option 3, but its dependence on state VET systems and private co-investment makes a staged commitment more prudent than immediate rollout.

**Sequence.** Timing is tight. The Committee reports on 30 November 2026; the revised Priority List methodology commences 1 January 2027. Option 1 must be settled within that window or wait a further year. Options 2 and 3 can begin in the 2027–28 Budget cycle.

**Responsibilities.** DEWR administers the incentive criterion and holds the Priority List methodology. JSA supplies exposure scores and owns the monitoring series. Treasury confirms budget neutrality. State training authorities are essential partners for Option 3.

**Indicators.** Entry-level commencements in high-exposure occupations; the growth gap between most- and least-exposed occupations, currently 3.9 percentage points; publication of the monitoring series with regional, age and sex disaggregation; and electrotechnology training places created under Option 3.

---

## Risks & Mitigations

**Data.** The ABS Labour Force Survey modernisation suspended detailed industry and occupation tables between April and August 2026, and DEWR's evidence extends only to February 2026. This brief therefore relies on JSA and DEWR analysis rather than current ABS series. Option 2 is the structural remedy; in the interim, conclusions should be treated as provisional and revisited once September 2026 data resume.

**Timing.** If the Priority List methodology is settled before the Committee reports, Option 1 lapses for a year. DEWR should be advised of the criterion independently of the Committee's timetable.

**Implementation.** Monitoring risks becoming procedural. Tie publication to Budget cycles.
