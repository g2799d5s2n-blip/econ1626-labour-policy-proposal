# Labour Policy Proposal

## Executive Summary

Artificial intelligence is not producing aggregate job losses in Australia. It is producing a recomposition of employment that aggregate indicators are poorly equipped to detect.

Exposure research — ILO Working Paper 140 (2025) and the OECD AI Capability Gap index (2026) — identify transformation rather than displacement as the dominant mechanism. DEWR (2026) finds no economy-wide disruption and reports employment among 20-24 year olds as more resilient than among those aged 25 and over since late 2022. This need not rule out contraction at entry level in exposed occupations. If such occupations account for only a modest share of youth employment, their contraction would be difficult to detect in an age-based aggregate — particularly alongside derived demand from AI infrastructure, with 13,000 of the 72,000 additional electricians and technicians required by 2030 linked directly to data centre construction. Aggregate resilience may be a signature of recomposition rather than evidence against it.

Compositional problems require targeted instruments. Yet the 2026-27 Budget withdraws $266.2 million over four years from employer apprenticeship incentives. This proposal recommends redirecting that expenditure rather than creating new programs.

## Problem Definition & Context

On 20 August 2026 the Australian Parliament established a Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence, due to report on 30 November 2026. Its inquiry creates an immediate window in which evidence on AI and the labour market can shape Commonwealth policy settings.

The policy problem is one of measurement as much as of impact. AI is reshaping Australian work through several distinct channels, and the indicators currently available to government capture them unevenly.

Three channels matter. The first concerns whether AI automates or augments tasks within existing occupations. The second concerns wages, where exposure appears to attract a premium rather than a penalty. The third — the focus of this brief — concerns sectoral restructuring and the compression of entry-level recruitment in exposed occupations, alongside derived demand for manual trades from AI infrastructure.

Each channel carries distributional consequences that national aggregates obscure, across regions, age groups and gender. These are addressed in the analysis below.

## Analysis of Impacts

**Task automation and augmentation.** Two independent measurement approaches converge on the same conclusion. The ILO's refined index scores exposure at task level across ISCO-08 occupations (Gmyrek et al. 2025), while the OECD maps nine AI capability domains against occupational requirements to construct a capability gap (OECD 2026). Despite differing methods, both identify transformation of occupations rather than wholesale substitution as the dominant pattern, with clerical and routine information-processing work most exposed. Jobs and Skills Australia reaches a broadly consistent view for the Australian labour market.

**Wages.** Exposure currently attracts a premium rather than a penalty. PwC (2026) reports a wage premium of 62 per cent for AI-skilled roles, up from 57 per cent, with AI-related job advertisements rising from 20,000 to 41,000 across 2024-25. This premium is itself distributional: it widens the gap between workers who can access AI training and those who cannot.

**Sectoral restructuring and entry-level compression.** The aggregate evidence is more supportive of a compositional reading than it first appears. DEWR (2026) finds no broad AI-driven upheaval, but reports that employment in the most AI-exposed occupations grew by 5.6 per cent since November 2022, against 9.5 per cent in the least exposed. It reads this as an early indication of modest slowing in highly exposed occupations rather than proof of job loss.

Firm-level evidence is sharper. Jobs and Skills Australia (2025) records employers shifting recruitment toward more senior and experienced workers and away from junior staff. ReadyTech reported hiring fewer entry-level engineers as it deployed genAI coding assistants, shifting its junior-senior composition; a senior leader saw no business case for training junior engineers as before. JSA concludes that entry-level roles may be more likely to transform than diminish, while the technology sector may be among the first to restructure its entry-level intake.

Yet employment among 20-24 year olds has grown slightly faster than among those aged 25 and over. Age-based aggregates pool divergent sectoral trajectories; the absence of a youth-level signal is largely uninformative about occupational contraction.

**Derived demand from AI infrastructure.** Skilled manual trades record low direct exposure on task-based measures — electricians score approximately 2.8/10 on Jobs and Skills Australia's occupational exposure scale (JSA 2025). Low exposure, however, is not insulation: it means only that AI does not perform these tasks, not that AI leaves demand for them unchanged.

The mechanism runs through physical infrastructure. Deloitte Access Economics (2026) tracks 33 Australian data centre projects worth $104 billion, a fourfold increase in a single year. Building and operating that capacity requires electrical and mechanical trades at scale. The Powering Skills Organisation (2026) estimates 72,000 additional electricians and technicians will be needed by 2030, of which 13,000 are tied directly to data centre construction — against a Skill Level 3 vacancy fill rate of 55.1 per cent (Jobs and Skills Australia 2026).

**Distributional consequences.** The three channels do not fall evenly. Exposure is concentrated in clerical and routine information-processing work rather than in occupations requiring non-standardised physical activity, and that concentration carries a gender profile: men make up 43.7 per cent of Australia's most-exposed group but close to 70 per cent of the least exposed (DEWR 2026), consistent with the ILO's global finding of higher female exposure in high-income economies (Gmyrek et al. 2025).

By age, the aggregate is reassuring and uninformative: employment among 20-24 year olds has outpaced older cohorts, while sectoral trajectories within that figure diverge sharply.

Regional variation is material — JSA publishes employment-region data — but the trades shortage is national in scope.

## Policy Options

### Option 1: Incorporate AI exposure into the Apprenticeships Priority List methodology

**Mechanism.** The 2026-27 Budget revises the Priority List methodology and incentive rates from 1 January 2027, redirecting employer incentives toward small and medium employers and Group Training Organisations. That redirection operates on firm size and existing priority occupations; it does not account for AI exposure. This option adds exposure as a criterion, making entry-level placements in highly exposed occupations eligible for employer incentives.

**Target.** Employers hiring first-year entrants in exposed clerical, administrative and technical roles — where DEWR (2026) records employment growth of 5.6 per cent against 9.5 per cent in the least exposed.

**Cost.** No new appropriation: the criterion is applied within the reformed incentive structure taking effect on 1 January 2027, which narrows eligibility and returns $266.2 million over four years ($106.3 million annually thereafter) to budget.

**Assessment.** Efficient, since the methodology is already being rewritten and no new appropriation is required. Equity is favourable given female concentration in exposed occupations. The binding constraint is timing: the Committee reports 30 November 2026, weeks before commencement.

### Option 2: Establish recurrent AI exposure monitoring within Jobs and Skills Australia

**Mechanism.** Convert JSA's Generative AI Capacity Study from a one-off exercise into a standing annual series, combining occupational exposure scores with the Internet Vacancy Index and reporting entry-level vacancy trends disaggregated by region, age and sex. DEWR's monitoring framework already draws on both inputs; this option gives it a recurring mandate and a published cadence.

**Target.** Not workers directly, but the institutions setting policy: JSA, DEWR, Treasury and future parliamentary inquiries.

**Cost.** Approximately $15-25 million annually, against the $9.1 million per year in ongoing funding JSA already receives for its legislated labour market advice.

**Assessment.** Highly feasible, since the analytical infrastructure exists and requires extension rather than construction. Its weakness is directness: it improves the quality of future decisions without altering any current labour market outcome. It is best understood as a complement to Option 1, not a substitute.

### Option 3: Co-fund a data centre trades training pathway with industry

**Mechanism.** The constraint in AI infrastructure trades is training capacity rather than demand: employers are already competing for the same electricians and mechanical technicians. AirTrunk's programme — thirty mechanical and electrical apprentices from May 2024, four years to a TAFE qualification, supplemented by the DCD>Academy training programme — demonstrates a workable model at small scale. This option co-funds its expansion, with Commonwealth contribution matched by participating operators and delivered through state TAFE systems.

**Target.** New entrants to electrical and mechanical trades, oriented toward data centre construction and operation — against the 13,000 additional workers tied to that pipeline by 2030.

**Cost.** Roughly $40-60 million over four years, between the two options above.

**Assessment.** Well targeted, but the weakest on feasibility: VET delivery is a state responsibility and the model depends on private operators sustaining co-investment. Deadweight loss is the principal risk, since operators are already recruiting and training without Commonwealth support.

## Recommended Package & Implementation Plan

**Recommendation.** Adopt Options 1 and 2 as an integrated package. Option 3 should not proceed as proposed: data centre operators are already recruiting and training without Commonwealth support, and co-funding a pathway the market is financing would deliver poor value. Its underlying constraint — TAFE delivery capacity — is better raised with states through existing National Skills Agreement mechanisms.

**Sequence.** The timing is unusually tight. The Committee reports on 30 November 2026; the revised Priority List methodology and incentive rates commence on 1 January 2027. Option 1 must therefore be settled within that window or wait a further year. Option 2 carries no such constraint and can begin in the 2027-28 Budget cycle.

**Responsibilities.** DEWR administers the incentive criterion and holds the Priority List methodology. JSA supplies exposure scores and would own the monitoring series. Treasury confirms budget neutrality.

**Cost.** Option 1 requires no new appropriation. Option 2 costs $15-25 million annually.

**Indicators.** Entry-level commencements in high-exposure occupations; the gap in employment growth between most- and least-exposed occupations, currently 3.9 percentage points; and publication of the monitoring series with regional, age and sex disaggregation.

## Risks & Mitigations

**Data.** The ABS Labour Force Survey modernisation suspended detailed industry and occupation tables between April and August 2026, and DEWR's evidence extends only to February 2026. This brief therefore relies on JSA and DEWR analysis rather than current ABS series. Option 2 is the structural remedy; in the interim, conclusions should be treated as provisional and revisited once September 2026 data resume.

**Timing.** If the Priority List methodology is settled before the Committee reports, Option 1 lapses for a year. DEWR should be advised of the criterion independently of the Committee's full reporting timetable.

**Implementation.** Monitoring risks becoming procedural. Tie publication to Budget cycles.
